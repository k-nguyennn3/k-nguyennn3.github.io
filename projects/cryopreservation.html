Cryopreservation of Sperm, Oocytes & Embryos

A literature review comparing the two ways reproductive cells are frozen, vitrification and slow freezing, and why each cell type's size and water content decide which one works.

The question

Infertility affects about 1 in 6 adults worldwide, and freezing sperm, eggs, and embryos lets patients preserve fertility before cancer treatment or delay family planning. Freezing living cells is at heart a heat- and mass-transfer problem. Cool too slowly and ice crystals grow and water is pulled out of the cells; cool too fast without enough cryoprotectant and ice forms inside them. We reviewed how each cell type is best frozen, and why.

What we covered

For each cell type we compared two protocols: slow, controlled-rate freezing, and vitrification (ultra-fast cooling in concentrated cryoprotectants that turns the cell's contents to a glass instead of ice). For each protocol we covered the cryoprotectant recipe, the cooling and warming rates, how cells are damaged (ice, osmotic, and oxidative stress), and clinical outcomes.

Cell typeWhy it's hard (or easy)Typical approach
SpermVery small with little cytoplasm and a high surface-to-volume ratio, so water moves out quickly and osmotic swings are tolerated.Slow freezing with 5–10% glycerol is the clinical standard; vitrification is still mostly in the lab.
Oocytes (eggs)One very large, water-rich cell with a meiotic spindle that is sensitive to cold between 25 °C and 4 °C.Vitrification at about −2,500 °C/min with ethylene glycol, DMSO, and sugars.
EmbryosMany cells plus, at the blastocyst stage, a fluid-filled cavity that responds strongly to osmotic changes.Vitrification (above 20,000 °C/min) has largely replaced slow freezing with seeding at −7 °C.

Key findings

  • Vitrified oocytes survive at roughly 90–95%, versus about 60% after slow freezing, so vitrification is now the standard for eggs.
  • Vitrified embryos exceed 90% survival after warming. A simplified one-step dehydration raised slow-freezing survival from 83.1% to 86.9% in one study.
  • Frozen sperm lose some motility but keep their ability to fertilize. Live births have been reported from sperm stored for 21 and 28 years.
  • Warming matters as much as cooling: rapid warming at 37 °C avoids ice recrystallization as the sample passes back through the danger zone.

These figures come from the studies we reviewed, not from original experiments.
